## How to review this

**Why English only.** A finished guide needs every string in **four languages** (en / fil / zh / id) —
`title`, `description`, `body`, `faq`, `related`. Translating four versions of a text you have not yet
approved is wasted work, and a change to the English invalidates all four. So: **approve the English
first, then I translate.** This is the same order we used for the 38 words.

**What I need you to correct.** Every passage marked ⚠️ is a claim I am **not confident about**, and
several are the kind of thing an outsider gets wrong while sounding certain. Please mark:
- ✅ correct · ✏️ correct-but-different-words · ❌ wrong (and what is right)

**The most important category is ❌.** A wrong etiquette claim does not just look bad — it causes the
exact awkwardness it was meant to prevent, and the helper has no way to know the app was wrong.

**On the Cantonese.** Each term appears with jyutping. Every one needs checking. Where I was unsure of
the jyutping I have written **(?) after it** rather than guess — those are the ones to look at first.

# PART 1 — Hong Kong customs & culture

**Proposed slug:** `hong-kong-customs-for-helpers`
**Proposed title:** *Hong Kong Customs and Culture: What a Helper Needs to Know*
**Proposed description:** *The unwritten rules of a Hong Kong household — the altar, the mahjong
table, the food you are offered, and the things that look like clutter but must not be moved.*

**Why this exists.** CantoBuddy teaches words. But some of the hardest moments in a Hong Kong
household are not a vocabulary problem — you know the words and still get it wrong. This is the page
for those.

---

## Draft body — English

### Opening

<p>Most of the difficult moments in a Hong Kong household are not a language problem. You can know
every word for <em>altar</em> and still do the wrong thing with it. This guide covers the unwritten
rules — the things nobody explains, because everyone assumes you already know.</p>

<p>None of this is a rule you will be tested on. It is background, so that a strange-looking object
in the flat is never a mystery and a polite gesture never goes unexplained.</p>

### 1. The things that are not clutter

<p>This is the most important section in the guide. A Hong Kong flat often contains objects that look
untidy or broken and are neither:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>神枱</strong> (san4 toi2) — the altar. A red shelf or cabinet, often near the entrance
    or in the living room, holding small figures, a photograph, an incense holder and sometimes fruit.
    <strong>Do not move it, do not clean it, and never throw anything from it away.</strong> If it
    needs dusting, ask first.</li>
  <li><strong>香爐</strong> (hoeng1 lou4) — the incense urn, usually on the altar. ⚠️ Ash is
    <em>not</em> dirt. Do not empty it neatly the way you would empty an ashtray.</li>
  <li><strong>Joss paper and paper offerings</strong> — printed paper, often gold or silver, sometimes
    in a stack or a bag. ⚠️ These look exactly like rubbish. They are not. If you find a bag of
    printed paper you do not recognise, leave it where it is and ask.</li>
  <li><strong>The doorway threshold</strong> and anything placed across it.</li>
</ul>

<p>Household guests and helpers take their shoes off at the door. ⚠️ There is usually a shoe rack or a
pair of slippers for you — if you are not sure, look for what everyone else does and copy it.</p>

### 2. Giving and receiving

<p>There is one habit worth learning on your first day, because it applies everywhere:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Give and receive with both hands.</strong> ⚠️ Handing something over — a red packet, a
    bowl, a cup of tea, a document — with one hand can read as casual or dismissive. Two hands, or
    your right hand supported at the wrist by your left, is the safe version.</li>
  <li><strong>Never point at a person with one finger.</strong> ⚠️ Point with an open hand instead.</li>
  <li><strong>唔該</strong> (m4 goi1) and <strong>多謝</strong> (do1 ze6) are not
    interchangeable, and getting it right matters. 唔該 is for a service — please and thank you for
    something someone <em>does</em>. 多謝 is for a gift — something someone <em>gives</em> you. Both are
    in the app: 唔該 is <a href="/words/please-excuse-me">here</a> and 多謝 <a href="/words/thank-you">here</a>.</li>
</ul>

### 3. Food, and why refusing is normal

<p>Food is how a Hong Kong household shows care, and it comes with its own grammar:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>食咗飯未</strong> (sik6 zo2 faan6 mei6) — "Have you eaten?" ⚠️ <strong>This is a
    greeting, not a dinner invitation.</strong> An elderly person especially may say it many times a
    day. The expected answer is not a full account of what you ate — a cheerful
    <strong>食咗喇</strong> (sik6 zo2 laa3) or "not yet" is enough. It is already in the app as a
    Greetings word.</li>
  <li><strong>Being offered food three times.</strong> ⚠️ In many households a first refusal is
    politeness. If you would like to accept, the safe move is to accept on the second or third
    offer, with 唔該.</li>
  <li><strong>Eat with the family, or after?</strong> ⚠️ This varies by household and there is no
    general rule. Watch what the family does in the first week, or simply ask —
    <strong>我可以一齊食嗎？</strong> (ngo5 ho2 ji5 jat1 cai4 sik6 maa3)</li>
  <li><strong>Chopsticks</strong> — ⚠️ do not stand them upright in a bowl of rice. <strong>(This
    one I want confirmed before it is published)</strong> — it is widely said to imitate incense at a
    funeral, but I would not print it without a native speaker confirming it is a real concern in
    ordinary households here rather than a general superstition.</li>
  <li><strong>長輩</strong> (zoeng2 bui3) — elders. ⚠️ Serve the eldest first, and greet them first
    when you come into the room.</li>
</ul>

### 4. The mahjong table

<p>If the family plays mahjong, there is one rule that matters and it is not about the game:</p>

<p><strong>Do not tidy a mahjong table while it is set up.</strong> ⚠️ A table mid-game is not
mess — the tiles are in a position that means something to the players, and the money on the table
is usually real. If the table needs cleaning, wait until the game has finished and been cleared by
the family.</p>

### 5. In the street and in the lift

<ul>
  <li><strong>Temples, the wet market, and Central on a Sunday.</strong> Sunday is the helper's day
    off, and Central fills with thousands of people. ⚠️ A picnic with cardboard screens on a
    footbridge is normal and legal at weekends.</li>
  <li><strong>Lifts and doorways.</strong> ⚠️ Let people out before you go in; hold the lift door for
    an elder.</li>
  <li><strong>Queueing.</strong> Hong Kong queues properly — for the MTR, the bus, a lift. Joining the
    back of the queue is a small thing that marks you as someone who knows how things work here.</li>
  <li><strong>Money.</strong> ⚠️ Hand notes and cards with both hands, as above. If you receive money
    that is not yours to keep, ask before assuming.</li>
</ul>

### 6. What to do when you are not sure

<p>There is one Cantonese sentence that solves more of these situations than any other:</p>

<p><strong>唔好意思，我唔知道<br>(m4 hou2 ji3 si1, ngo5 m4 zi1 dou6)</strong><br>
"Sorry, I didn't know."</p>

<p>Say it honestly and calmly, and in almost every case the answer will be a shrug and an
explanation — because the expectation is not that a new helper knows everything on day one. It is
that she asks.</p>

## Draft FAQ — English

**Q. I moved something on the altar to clean. What should I do?**
A. Put it back as closely as you can, and tell the family — plainly and early — that you moved it
while cleaning. ⚠️ Do not just quietly replace it and hope. In most households saying so is the end
of it; hiding it is what turns it into a problem. **This answer needs the reviewer's confirmation
that "say so plainly" is the right advice and not an over-reaction.**

**Q. Is it rude to refuse food?**
A. Not if you refuse politely. You can always say **我夠喇，唔該** (ngo5 gau3 laa3, m4 goi1) — "I've had
enough, thank you." Being offered repeatedly is usually care, not pressure.

**Q. How do I know if something is an offering or rubbish?**
A. If in doubt, it is not rubbish. Printed paper, ash, fruit on a small shelf, and anything with
incense near it should be left alone and asked about.

**Q. Do I have to take part in religious practices?**
A. No. ⚠️ **Reviewer: please confirm.** My understanding is that a helper is not expected to worship,
only to be careful around the objects and not to obstruct or dispose of them. I would like this
answered well, because it is the question most likely to worry someone.

# PART 2 — Hong Kong festivals

**Proposed slug:** `hong-kong-festivals` *(no year, deliberately — see the date rule)*
**Proposed title:** *Hong Kong Festivals: A Guide for Helpers — What Changes at Home*
**Proposed description:** *Lunar New Year, Mid-Autumn, Ching Ming and the rest — what each festival
means, what arrives in the house, and what you should not do.*

**Why this one is stronger than Part 1.** A custom is background. A **festival is dated and
practical** — it tells you what is about to happen in your household, what will arrive, and what you
will be asked to do. That is usable in advance, which is the main thing a helper needs.

---

## ⚠️ READ FIRST: the date rule

**This guide names no dates, and that is deliberate.**

Lunar New Year, Mid-Autumn and Chung Yeung follow the **lunisolar calendar**. The date changes every
year — Lunar New Year falls somewhere between late January and late February. A guide that says
"Lunar New Year is on 17 February" becomes **wrong within twelve months**, and nothing in the app
would notice or warn anyone. A wrong date is worse than no date, because someone may plan a whole
month around it.

So every reference is written as a **window plus a check**:

> *"In late January or February, depending on the lunar calendar. The family will know the exact
> dates — ask them at the start of the month, because this is one of the busiest times of the year
> in the household."*

That version never goes stale. **If you would rather have real dates, that is possible, but it needs
a small table of festival dates that someone updates every year — and nothing in the app does that
today.** My recommendation is the window. Tell me if you disagree.

## Draft body — English

### Opening

<p>Hong Kong's year is marked by festivals, and several of them change what happens in the house you
work in. This guide is not about history or religion. It is about the practical side: <strong>what
arrives, what changes, and what you should not do.</strong></p>

<p>Dates follow the lunar calendar and move each year, so this guide deliberately gives you the
season rather than a date. The family always knows the exact days — and asking is a good
conversation to have at the start of the month.</p>

### 1. Lunar New Year — 新年 (san1 nin2)

**When:** late January or February. The biggest festival of the year, and the busiest week in a Hong
Kong household.

<p><strong>What changes at home:</strong></p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>The deep clean happens BEFORE, not during.</strong> The whole flat is cleaned in the
    days before the new year.</li>
  <li><strong>⚠️ Do not sweep or clean on the first day.</strong> The usual explanation is that
    sweeping sweeps the year's good fortune out of the house. Whatever the reason, it is one of the
    few rules that is enforced and it is easy to break by accident, because you are trying to be
    helpful. <strong>The app already has 掃地 (sou2 dei2, "sweep the floor") and 抹枱 (maat3 toi2,
    "wipe the table") — so this is a direct, practical warning about words she already knows.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>利是</strong> (lai6 si6) — red packets. Small red envelopes with money, given to
    children, to unmarried people, and often to helpers. ⚠️ If one is given to you, receive it with
    <strong>both hands</strong> and say <strong>多謝</strong> (not 唔該 — it is a gift).</li>
  <li><strong>Visiting.</strong> Relatives visit, usually in the first days. There is often a lot of
    food and a lot of tea, and the kettle runs constantly.</li>
  <li><strong>⚠️ Fruit and sweet boxes</strong> arrive as gifts and are usually <strong>re-gifted</strong>,
    not eaten. Ask before opening a box that is still wrapped or ribboned.</li>
  <li><strong>Flowers and plants</strong> — ⚠️ a lucky plant (often 桔, a small kumquat tree) is
    bought for the festival and is <strong>not</strong> to be thrown out or moved during it.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Useful words:</strong>
恭喜發財 (gung1 hei2 faat3 coi4) — the standard new-year greeting, "wishing you prosperity";
新年快樂 (san1 nin2 faai3 lok6) — Happy New Year;
利是 (lai6 si6) — red packet.
<strong>⚠️ Reviewer: all four jyutping readings need checking.</strong></p>

### 2. Mid-Autumn Festival — 中秋節 (zung1 cau1 zit3)

**When:** September or early October.

<ul>
  <li><strong>月餅</strong> (jyut6 beng2) — mooncakes. Dense, expensive, and given as gifts in
    decorated tins.</li>
  <li>⚠️ <strong>Do not open a mooncake tin early.</strong> The tins are usually meant to be given on
    or near the day. A cake that has been cut into may no longer be givable.</li>
  <li>⚠️ <strong>Do not throw away a tin or its box.</strong> Tins are often kept and reused, and
    some are valuable.</li>
  <li><strong>Lanterns</strong> — children carry them in the evening. Often a family outing.</li>
  <li>The family may go out at night to see the moon. ⚠️ Expect to be asked to help with the
    preparation and possibly to stay late.</li>
</ul>

### 3. Ching Ming — 清明 (cing1 ming4)

**When:** early April.

<ul>
  <li>A <strong>grave-visiting</strong> festival. The family goes to clean and tend an ancestor's
    grave and to make offerings.</li>
  <li>⚠️ <strong>The household may be out for most of the day</strong>, often including travel to the
    mainland or the New Territories. Expect a full day, and plan meals and the elder's care around it.</li>
  <li>⚠️ Offerings and paper goods for this are bought in advance and kept in the flat. They are not
    rubbish — see Part 1, §1.</li>
</ul>

### 4. Chung Yeung — 重陽 (cung4 joeng4)

**When:** September or October, close to Mid-Autumn.

<ul>
  <li>Also a grave-tending festival, and a public holiday.</li>
  <li>⚠️ ⚠️ <strong>Reviewer: I want to be careful here.</strong> Many families climb a hill or a
    mountain on this day, and the day is sometimes described as an autumn outing. I am not confident
    how commonly a family would expect a helper to come along versus have the day off. <strong>Please
    correct this section rather than let me guess.</strong></li>
</ul>

### 5. Tuen Ng — Dragon Boat Festival — 端午節 (dyun1 ng5 zit3)

**When:** May or June.

<ul>
  <li><strong>糉</strong> (zung2) — rice dumplings, wrapped in leaves. Glutinous rice with pork or
    beans, steamed. ⚠️ They arrive as gifts, in quantity, and they are <strong>filling</strong> — one
    is a meal.</li>
  <li>Dragon boat races are the public side and are usually on television or at the harbour.</li>
  <li>⚠️ Storage: they are usually kept in the fridge and re-steamed, not eaten cold. Check with the
    family rather than assuming.</li>
</ul>

### 6. Winter Solstice — 冬至 (dung1 zi3)

**When:** usually 21 or 22 December.

<ul>
  <li>⚠️ <strong>This one is easy for an outsider to underestimate. In many Cantonese families the
    winter-solstice dinner is treated as more important than Lunar New Year.</strong> The whole family
    gathers, and the meal matters.</li>
  <li><strong>做冬</strong> (zou6 dung1) — "celebrating the solstice" — is the phrase for the family
    dinner.</li>
  <li>It is <strong>not</strong> a public holiday, so the household may still be working while
    preparing for it. Expect a long day.</li>
</ul>

### 7. Household observances you may see

<ul>
  <li>Incense, offerings of fruit, and paper goods at an altar, on the first and fifteenth of the
    lunar month and on other days.</li>
  <li>⚠️ <strong>You will not always be told these are happening.</strong> They are private. The only
    thing to get right is Part 1, §1: do not move, tidy or discard anything involved.</li>
</ul>

### What to do when a festival is coming

<ol>
  <li><strong>Ask at the start of the month.</strong>
    <strong>嚟緊有咩節日要準備？</strong> (lai4 gan6 jau5 me1 zit3 jat6 jiu3 zeon2 bei6) —
    "Is there a festival coming that we need to prepare for?" <em>(⚠️ need checking)</em></li>
  <li><strong>Write down what is expected</strong> — the cleaning, the cooking, whether the family
    goes out, whether you go with them.</li>
  <li><strong>Ask before throwing anything away</strong> in the week before and after a festival. This
    is exactly when objects have a purpose you cannot see.</li>
</ol>

## Draft FAQ — English

**Q. Why can't I sweep on Lunar New Year's first day?**
A. ⚠️ The usual reason given is that sweeping on the first day sweeps away the year's good luck, so
the cleaning is all done beforehand. Whatever you believe about it, it is the household's custom and
breaking it is noticed. **Reviewer: is this the right way to phrase it for a helper? I have tried to
explain without endorsing, because she may not share the belief.**

**Q. What do I say if I receive a red packet?**
A. <strong>多謝</strong> with both hands. If the family gives one to your own children or family,
唔該／多謝 as the family would use it — but for money received by <em>you</em>, 多謝 is the right one.

**Q. Do the dates change every year?**
A. Yes. Lunar New Year, Mid-Autumn, Chung Yeung and Tuen Ng all follow the lunar calendar and move.
Ching Ming is on or around 4–5 April and the winter solstice on 21–22 December, which are far more
fixed. **The app gives you the season and asks you to confirm with the family.**

**Q. Will I get the festival off?**
A. ⚠️ **Reviewer: I have deliberately not answered this.** In Hong Kong, the statutory holidays for
a foreign domestic helper are specified by law, and I do not want to state something wrong about
entitlements in an app people may rely on. It is a real question and it deserves a correct answer —
either from someone who knows the law, or by saying plainly that the family and the contract decide.
**Tell me which you prefer.**

**Every festival word is missing.** So the answer to "should this be a category?" is now a

1. **The culture text is a guide** — unchanged. A custom is not sayable, so it cannot be a word card.

   real quiz.

I had it half wrong in the earlier proposal. The distinction is not *culture vs vocabulary*, it is:
**sayable → word card. Not sayable → guide.** A festival has both, and should get both.

**I have not added any words**, because adding a category means a `CONTENT_VERSION` bump and a
